# Pharmaceuticals: the clinical rules in plain language (M42, for Reece)

Drugs reach the market in the game by the rules below. Please mark anything that rings false.

## 1. A programme and its molecule

- A **programme** is a molecule aimed at one of **20 indications** in seven areas (oncology, metabolic and obesity, immunology, neuroscience, infectious disease and vaccines, cardiovascular, rare disease), as a small molecule, a biologic or a cell or gene therapy.
- Every molecule has a **true efficacy and safety that nobody ever sees**. Each readout observes efficacy with noise, so the screen shows an **estimate as a range** that narrows with each trial (wide after Phase I, tight after Phase III).
- **Stages:** discovery (about 4.5 years), preclinical (1 year; the composition patent is filed at its start), Phase I (about 2.3 years, 20–100 patients), Phase II (3.6 years, 100–300), Phase III (3.3 years, 300–2,000), then filing and review. The player picks each trial's design: lean (fewer patients, shorter, a slightly lower chance), standard, or large.
- **Costs** follow published medians: about $3.4M for a Phase I, $8.6M for a Phase II and about $50M for a Phase III ($41,000 a patient), more in oncology (x1.6) and for biologics (x1.3) and cell and gene therapies (x2). Like sales, costs are at the game's scale (8.5% of real).

## 2. The odds

- Each readout's chance starts from the **published success rate for its area and phase** (BIO 2011–2020): overall Phase I→II 52%, II→III 29%, III→filing 58%, filing→approval 91%, so about **8% of Phase I programmes are approved**. By area: rare disease 17%, metabolic 15.5%, infectious disease 13%, immunology 11%, neuroscience 6.5%, oncology 5.3%, cardiovascular 4.8%.
- The molecule's hidden efficacy and safety move that chance up or down: Phase I is mostly about safety, Phases II and III mostly about efficacy. The screen shows each chance as a range, never the number behind it.
- **A failed readout** leaves the choice to **redesign** (run the phase again at its cost plus 20%, with a fresh result) or **stop**.
- In the game's tests, 11.6% of a cohort spread evenly over the seven areas reached approval (the areas' own rates average 10.4%), oncology 48% below the average, and Phase I to approval took a median of **10.2 years**.

## 3. AI discovery

- From **AlphaFold 2 (November 2020)**, a company with a strong AI model (quality 60+) or drug-discovery research can run AI discovery: more programmes in discovery at once (+50%), **a preclinical stage 30% shorter**, and a small lift at Phase I and II. AI-discovered molecules so far pass Phase I 80–90% of the time but Phase II only about 40% (BCG 2024), so the lift is kept modest: in tests AI gives about **25% more approvals per dollar**.
- An AI discovery platform can be licensed to a rival for $2M a month (real money, scaled like everything else) for three years.

## 4. Approval

| Regulator | Application | Standard | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FDA (US) | NDA (small molecules) / BLA (biologics) | 11–13 months | 7–9 months |
| EMA (EU) | MAA | 13–17 months | 9–12 months |
| PMDA (Japan) | J-NDA | 11–14 months | 8–10 months |
| NMPA (China) | NDA | 14–24 months | 10–14 months (from 2017) |

- **Priority review** comes with an efficacy estimate clearly above the standard of care, an orphan indication or breakthrough designation. **Breakthrough** (from July 2012) follows a strong Phase II and shortens Phase III by 20%. **Accelerated approval** (US, oncology and rare disease, a very strong Phase II) allows filing after Phase II; a confirmatory Phase III starts at approval and, if it fails, the US approval is withdrawn.
- An **advisory committee** meets half way on about 20% of first US filings; a vote against makes approval much less likely. A refusal is a **complete response letter**; a resubmission is possible but less likely to succeed.
- Other regions **register by reliance** on an FDA or EU approval within 6–18 months. Where prices go through **HTA** (Europe, Japan and China, Australia), a decision takes 12–24 months; a list price above the drug's value cap is not reimbursed, and the drug then reaches only about 15% of patients there.

## 5. Exclusivity and the cliff

- **US:** the patent's 20 years from filing plus Hatch-Waxman's restoration (half the trial years plus the review, at most 5 years and at most 14 years after approval), against 5 years' data exclusivity (12 for biologics) and 7 for orphans. **EU:** 8 + 2 years (+1 for a significant benefit), the patent plus an SPC (at most 15 years after approval), orphans 10. **Elsewhere:** the patent and 6 years.
- **The cliff:** when exclusivity ends in a region, a small molecule keeps about 10% of its patients within months (generics 85% cheaper: the FDA finds prices 79% lower with four generics, 95% with six); a biologic keeps about 64% after a year and 45% after two (as Humira did). In tests a small-molecule blockbuster lost **88% of its revenue** within 24 months.

## 6. Prices and the IRA

- The player sets a drug's **US list price**; the net price is list less the area's rebates (about 50% on average; 65% for diabetes and obesity, 25% for oncology). Other regions pay a share of the US net price: Europe 35%, East Asia 30%, Oceania 40%, Latin America 25%, the rest 10% (US prices are about 2.8x the OECD's).
- A drug's share of its indication's treated patients grows with its efficacy and safety over the standard of care, its months since launch (about 5 years to peak), its medical-affairs and sales support, and, in the US, a lower list price.
- **The Inflation Reduction Act:** Medicare negotiates prices 9 years after approval (13 for biologics; orphan drugs exempt). The first 10 prices apply from January 2026 (about 22% lower), 15 more from 2027 (44%), then 15 and 20 a year with seeded discounts; each year after 2025 there is a 15% chance small molecules move to 13 years too. The discount applies to the drug's Medicare share of US patients.

## 7. Making drugs, licensing and rivals

- Drugs are made in an **own plant** (an API plant: $250M, 30 months; a 60 kL biologics plant: $450M, 42 months) or by a **contract manufacturer** (Lonza, Samsung Biologics, WuXi Biologics, Catalent) at a higher cost a patient. Plants' quality levels drive GMP inspections (warning letters, import bans, consent decrees) and recalls, as for devices.
- **Licensing:** each January biotechs offer about six programmes; in-license one (upfront, milestones at Phase II, Phase III and approval, and a royalty) or buy it outright. The player can also out-license a programme to a rival or co-develop it 50/50.
- **Rivals:** Pfizer, J&J, Roche, Merck, AbbVie, Novartis, AstraZeneca, Novo Nordisk, Eli Lilly, BMS, Sanofi and GSK, plus the generic makers Teva, Sandoz, Viatris and Sun. Their revenue to 2025 is their reported revenue (Pfizer $67.8B in 2010, Lilly $65.2B in 2025), with key drugs and their loss-of-exclusivity dates (Keytruda 2028, Eliquis 2028, semaglutide 2031). After 2025 their portfolios age, their drugs meet their cliffs and the IRA, and they approve about two new drugs a year for every $50B of revenue. The player's drugs take patients from rivals' drugs for the same indication.
