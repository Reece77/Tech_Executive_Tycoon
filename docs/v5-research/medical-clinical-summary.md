# Medical devices: the clinical rules in plain language (M41, for Reece)

Medical devices reach the market in the game by the rules below. Please mark anything that rings false.

## 1. What a device is

- **Five kinds of device**, each its own market:
  - wearables and diagnostics (CGMs, ECG watches, home tests);
  - imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI);
  - surgical robots;
  - implants (pacemakers and defibrillators, neurostimulators, orthopaedic implants);
  - software and AI as a medical device.
- **The intended use sets the risk class**, by region. For example, a CGM was a US class III device until Dexcom's G6 De Novo (March 2018) created the class II "iCGM" type. Software was mostly EU class I under the old directive (MDD) and is mostly class IIa or IIb under the MDR's rule 11.
- **Rivals' devices are treated as cleared** wherever history sold them. Twelve rivals are in the game: Medtronic, J&J MedTech, Abbott, Stryker, Boston Scientific, Siemens Healthineers, GE HealthCare, Philips, Intuitive, Dexcom, Edwards and BD.

## 2. Routes to market (US)

| Route | When | Review time in the game | Approval chance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exempt | Most class I | registration only | — |
| 510(k) | Class II with a predicate (any company's device of that use already cleared) | 3–4 months (3–5 before MDUFA IV), plus 1–3 months when the FDA asks for more information, which it does for about 65% of files | about 92% |
| De Novo | Novel class II, no predicate | 8–12 months, plus 2–4 more on a request; 12–18 before FDASIA's direct De Novo (July 2012) | about 60% |
| PMA | Class III; needs a successful pivotal study first | 9–12 months, plus 3–8 months for a major deficiency or panel (about half) | about 88% |

- A granted De Novo becomes the predicate for everyone after it.
- Approval chances rise with device quality and evidence.

## 3. Routes to market (EU and elsewhere)

- **EU:**
  - class I self-declares;
  - everything else needs a notified body: 5–9 months under the MDD (to April 2021), 11–16 months under the MDR (from 26 May 2021);
  - class III and implants add four months for the clinical evaluation consultation, and need a pivotal study under the MDR.
- **Japan (PMDA):** 9–13 months for a new device, 3–5 months with an approved equivalent.
- **China (NMPA):** 8–14 months, and almost always a deficiency round.
- **Other regions** (Canada and Mexico, the UK and non-EU Europe, Korea, Latin America, South and Southeast Asia, Africa, Oceania): register in 3–12 months by relying on an FDA or CE clearance.

## 4. Clinical evidence

- **Three study types:**
  - feasibility: 20–60 patients, 9–15 months;
  - pivotal: 147–415 patients, the IQR around Rathi's median of 241; 18–36 months; De Novo pivotal studies are smaller, at 60–180 patients;
  - post-market: 300–1,000 patients, 24–48 months.
- **Cost per patient:** from about $3,000 (a software reader study) to $40,000 (an implant), spent month by month.
- **The result is unknown until readout.** Before then the screen shows only a range for the chance of meeting the endpoint: about 67% for a pivotal study at average quality, more with better devices and earlier evidence.
- **A failure** costs a 6-month redesign and 20% of the study's cost again.
- **Evidence strength** (0–100%) comes from studies that met their endpoint; a pivotal study adds 60 points. It raises approval, coverage and adoption.

## 5. Reimbursement and adoption

- **Without payer coverage** a hospital device sells only to early adopters, about 15% of demand.
- **Coverage requests by region:**
  - US Medicare decides in 9–72 months, the higher end without evidence (novel devices took a median 5.7 years, and only 44% were ever covered);
  - Europe's national health technology assessments take 12–36 months.
- **Chance of coverage:** from 30% with no evidence to 90% with full evidence.
- **TCET** (from August 2024): a US device with a pivotal study and a novel route can be decided in 6 months.
- **Clinician adoption** follows an S-curve from 3% at clearance:
  - without evidence or training, it takes about 9 years to reach 30%;
  - a successful pivotal study cuts that to about 4 years;
  - paid clinical education ($50,000 a month per region for the full effect) speeds it further.
- **Consumer devices** have no adoption curve and need no coverage.

## 6. Quality, inspections and recalls

- **Each device plant runs a quality system** at one of three levels, costing $10k, $40k or $120k a month. Best practice has about a third of the standard defect rate; minimal has twice it.
- **Inspections:** about one every two years per regulator, scaled by its strictness. At standard quality the findings are:

| Finding | Chance | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observations (Form 483) | 35% | none if corrected |
| Warning letter | 6% | holds that regulator's decisions for 6 months, and brand loss |
| Import ban | 1% | stops sales there for 9 months |
| Consent decree | 0.3% | stops sales for 24 months and costs 5% of a year's device revenue (Philips, 2024) |

- **Recalls:** about 12% of marketed device lines a year at standard quality, more with a large installed base.
  - The class mix is the FDA's: 9% class I, 88% class II, 3% class III.
  - Post-market surveillance ($15k a month) catches 60% of defects early, dropping the recall one class.
  - Each class has a field-action cost (60%, 12% and 2% of the installed units' price), a stop on new sales (3, 1 and 0 months) and brand loss.
  - A class I recall also adds lawsuits and costs clinician trust. It is a critical event with three answers: recall every unit (some brand back), field correction (cost back, brand down), or dispute the class (more cost back, but a warning letter).
- **Surgical robots earn about $57,000 a month per installed system** in instruments and service (Intuitive's 2024 figure). A robotics entrant therefore earns most of its revenue from its installed base after a few years.
